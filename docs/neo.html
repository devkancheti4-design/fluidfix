A repair tool whose every decision is a ruling

The brain is six integer laws. It has never ruled wrong. Put your hand on the bits.

Every decision fluidfix makes comes from a machine-authored kernel: eight measured bits in, one act out, re-derived over its entire input space on every run. The panel below is the real engine law, all 256 situations, exactly as it ships.

This is the shipped kernel's full truth table, generated from fluidfix.engine.decide over 0..255. SHIP is ruled on exactly four of 256 bytes. Adding any evidence bit can only withdraw a ship, never create one: 0 of 1,024 bit-flips turn a refusal into SHIP.

The brain never takes a wrong decision. Here is what that sentence means, exactly.

It does not mean fluidfix has never shipped a wrong repair. It has, and the record of every one is public. It means something narrower and stronger: across 63 recorded incidents, not one defect has ever lived in a ruling. Every wrong outcome traced to a bit measured wrongly, an act applied wrongly, or a report worded wrongly. Handed the honest byte, the law refuses every time.

That is why the design puts the decision in a kernel and the measurement in code. A kernel of 1,555 characters can be checked over every input it will ever see. Code that measures a situation cannot, so that is where the defects live, and that is where the work goes.

The trade you are making. A predictive model will produce an answer to any question. This will not. When it cannot prove the answer it refuses, names the bit it could not settle, and leaves your tree byte-identical. On weak test suites we measured that refusal saving you from a wrong program 15.4% of the time. That is the whole bargain: bounded reach in exchange for a decision you can audit.

Bounded reach. Knowledge is what you provide.

The brain rules. It does not know your codebase. What it repairs is whatever fault classes it has been taught, judged by whatever your suite can see. So the honest question is not "what can it do" but "how much of real bug-fixing sits inside that boundary". We measured it on three engines' full histories.

Read the columns right to left. The six classes that ship in the box cover two to four percent of real fix commits, consistently, across three codebases. That is the untaught floor. Teach classes shaped to your own regressions and the ceiling is the column to its left: more than half of all bug fixes on two of the three engines, if a class may span up to 25 lines in one file.

Why 25 and not 30: going from 25 to 30 buys at most 2.5 points. Going from 30 to unbounded buys the rest only by permitting arbitrarily large rewrites. So one repair may change at most 25 lines, and a class that asks for more is refused. That cap also closed a real hole: a taught class could once return a span covering the whole file, and a red team used it to flip an unrelated flag thirteen lines from the defect.

What binds Box2D is not shape or size. Fifty-nine percent of its fixes touch more than one file. No line budget reaches those. The law already has the ruling for that situation, called PARTITION: when the failing tests split into independent groups, repair them one at a time. It is verified over all 256 inputs. It is not yet actuated. That is the next thing we build, and it is the honest road from 35% to 85%.

How you give it examples.

You do not write rules. You write one incident down, once, as a register() call, and every future member of that class is decided by the kernel for free. The code below is executed by the test suite on every CI run, so if this page drifts from reality the build goes red.

# Taught from ONE worked example (incident INC-2041):
#   due += inv["amount"] + inv.get("tax")      <- summed None, crashed
#   due += inv["amount"] + inv.get("tax", 0)   <- the fix
register(
    4,
    "missing-get-default",
    'a .get(key) with no default, letting None poison arithmetic',
    re.compile(r"\.get\((\"[^\"]+\"|'[^']+')\)"),
    lambda line, o: re.sub(r"\.get\((\"[^\"]+\"|'[^']+')\)",
                           r".get(\1, 0)", line),
)

Five arguments. A slot number, a name, a one-line description, the signal that says "this line is a member", and the transform that produces the candidate. The router infers the act code from the same single worked example it always uses. You never touch the kernel.

Getting the most out of it.

Everything below is a measured lever, not advice.

Shape a class to what actually distinguishes it.

The same Box2D defect, twice. A class shaped to "an assignment" pointed everywhere, the search space exploded, and after 30 minutes it refused. A class shaped to the field-name pattern that set the bug apart repaired it in 34 suite runs and 116 seconds, deriving a field name it had never been shown. Same law, same engine, opposite outcome. Shaping is the single biggest lever on what this is worth.

Let your suite see the defect.

On cglm, three of six real historical bugs left all 1,131 tests passing. No repair tool can act on a failure that does not occur. fluidfix hotspots ranks your files by defect density in your own history and tells you which are one test away from being maintained; on Box2D, restricted to files that still exist, 60% of past defects live in 16 files.

Run it on every push, unattended.

A repair costs suite runs and nothing else, so there is no reason to ration it. A class you taught in 2016 will still be guarding in 2026. Everything it does is byte-exact, rolled back on rejection, and journaled across a crash. We measured the journal recovering a file on a real repository after a kill, unprompted.

Keep repairs small on purpose.

The 25-line cap is a ceiling, not a target. Most real fixes that sit inside one file are under three lines. A refusal that says "raise the budget" is the law telling you the search was cut short with a candidate in hand, and it will name the candidate.

What it costs, and why that is realistic.

One qualifying defect costs an engineer thirty to sixty minutes, call it $50 to $150. Multiply by how many you get a year, then by the fraction your suite catches. On a slow library that is a couple of hundred dollars and no licence at all. On a live-service title patching weekly it is plausibly ten to thirty thousand.
